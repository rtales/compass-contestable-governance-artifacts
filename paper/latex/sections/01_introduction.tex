\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\textbf{Why FAccT should care.}
Public education agencies increasingly deploy (or procure) predictive decision support to triage scarce interventions: tutoring slots, counseling capacity, special-needs assessments, dropout prevention programs, and scholarship support.
When these systems fail, the harm is not only predictive error---it is a \emph{crisis of legitimacy}: affected students and families cannot understand, contest, or obtain meaningful remedy.

Recent episodes illustrate this legitimacy gap.
In 2020, the UK A-level ``grading algorithm'' was rolled out with limited contestation capacity and generated large-scale, socially salient downgrades that triggered public backlash and a policy U-turn \cite{Wired2020AlevelAlgorithm}.
In U.S. K--12 settings, early-warning models have drawn scrutiny for inequitable risk assignments and opaque governance (e.g., Wisconsin's dropout early-warning system) \cite{Markup2023DEWS}.
And in adjacent public-sector risk profiling, courts have struck down systems for insufficient transparency and accountability (e.g., the Dutch SyRI ruling) \cite{SyRI2020Case}.
These cases are not outliers: they expose a recurring pattern where \emph{automation arrives before contestability and accountability}.

\textbf{From metrics to contestability.}
Responsible-AI frameworks and toolkits have matured rapidly, but many deployments still reduce governance to model-level metrics and checklists.
This creates a risk of \emph{fairwashing}: institutions claim ethical compliance via selective indicators while offering weak recourse, unclear responsibility for trade-offs, and poor auditability \cite{CitronPasquale2014ScoredSociety,Wieringa2020AccountFor,Metcalf2021AIA,Selbst2019Abstraction}.
In public education, where labels and historical measurements can embed structural inequities \cite{BakerHawn2022AlgorithmicBiasEducation}, governance must be treated as an institutional procedure, not a one-off model tuning exercise.

\textbf{Our approach: governing by artifacts.}
We propose \compa-\ecl-\mas, a governance-first layer that turns ethical requirements into \emph{versioned policy} and produces \emph{auditable artifacts} supporting oversight, appeals, and iterative revision.
The central design premise is simple: \emph{if a decision can be contested, the system must ship the artifacts required to contest it.}
Concretely, the framework (i) encodes multi-dimensional requirements as computable constraints, (ii) implements \emph{abstention as a procedural safeguard} for uncertain cases (routing to human review), and (iii) selects deployable configurations via an explicit, institution-defined policy over Pareto-feasible candidates.

\subsection{Contributions}

\paragraph{Scope.}
We make \emph{mechanism-level} claims about governance infrastructure: candidate sweeps, constraint checking, deterministic selection, artifact emission, and replayability for operational contestability.
We do \emph{not} claim downstream social impact (e.g., improved educational outcomes, reduced inequity in deployment, or field adoption).
Our artifacts are \emph{necessary but not sufficient} for substantive recourse: institutions still must provide accessible processes, capacity, and legal safeguards.

This paper contributes \emph{auditable infrastructure} for contestable public educational decision support:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Abstention as procedural safeguard:} We formalize a Top-$K$ decision rule with uncertainty-triggered abstention that creates a concrete pathway to human review for uncertain or policy-sensitive cases.
\item \textbf{Artifacts for contestability:} We define an artifact schema (decision logs, constraint reports, and policy manifests) that enables third-party audits, reconstruction of past decisions, and structured appeals.
\item \textbf{Policy versioning as transparency:} We operationalize institutional value choices as a \emph{versioned} policy (thresholds, guardrails, tie-breakers), enabling re-runs under new mandates without rewriting code.
\item \textbf{Constraint-based multi-stakeholder governance:} We show how multiple ethical dimensions (fairness, operational capacity, transparency signals, and safeguards) can be negotiated as constraints rather than hidden in optimizer weights.
\item \textbf{Socio-technical feasibility via trade-offs:} We provide a reproducible pipeline to surface the efficiency--fairness--oversight trade space, supporting deliberation rather than claiming a single ``best'' model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope and claims}
This is a \emph{systems} contribution: we do not claim a new fairness definition or a new learning algorithm.
Our claim is that contestability and accountability require \emph{procedural artifacts} and \emph{policy versioning} that are often missing from educational AI deployments.
We evaluate the framework through controlled stress tests and an artifact-centric contestability scorecard, and we provide a reproducible fixed-score analyses, a tested reference application, and versioned artifact definitions.

The reported predictors estimate correctness or student success. They are mechanism testbeds, not validated rules for assigning support to the students at greatest risk. A prospective early-warning use requires a separately specified target, horizon and evaluation.
